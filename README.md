# who-is-running

An open dataset of **who is running in US elections**, covering federal, state and local races. It's built only from free public sources and refreshed daily by GitHub Actions.

Each source writes one normalized CSV per year to `data/<source>/<year>.csv`, all with the same columns.

**Browse it:** https://alexandernicholasivanov.github.io/who-is-running/. Pick a state and year to see every race and who's running. Pick a county to see everything on that county's ballot: statewide races, the districts that include it, and its local races. Filter by level, primary/general, or a name or office search. Filtered views have shareable links, e.g. [`#state=NC&year=2026&county=WAKE`](https://alexandernicholasivanov.github.io/who-is-running/#state=NC&year=2026&county=WAKE).

## Sources

| Source | Coverage | Years | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| `civic` | **All states except NC, MD and VA**: state and local races on upcoming ballots, sampled by county | 2026–present (upcoming elections only) | [Google Civic Information API](https://developers.google.com/civic-information) (Voting Information Project data); needs an API key |
| `fec` | President, US Senate, US House, all states; DC's and the territories' House delegates | 2000–present (2-year cycles) | [FEC candidate master file](https://www.fec.gov/campaign-finance-data/candidate-master-file-description/) |
| `md` | Maryland: federal, state, legislative, judicial, county offices, school board, party central committees | 2022–present | [Maryland State Board of Elections candidate lists](https://elections.maryland.gov/candidacy/index.html) |
| `enr` | Colorado, Florida, Iowa, Minnesota, South Carolina, West Virginia: candidates for state, legislative, judicial and some local offices on the 2026 general ballot, and live, unofficial county results for statewide races | 2026 | State election offices' live results: [Clarity Election Night Reporting](https://results.enr.clarityelections.com/) sites (CO, IA, SC, WV), [Florida's](https://flelectionfiles.floridados.gov/enightfilespublic/) and [Minnesota's](https://electionresultsfiles.sos.mn.gov/) results files |
| `medsl` | **All 50 states + DC**: federal, state and local offices on the November ballot | 2016–2024 (even years) | [MIT Election Data + Science Lab precinct returns](https://github.com/MEDSL) |
| `nc` | North Carolina, every level: federal, state, judicial, county, municipal, school board, special districts | 2010–present | [NC State Board of Elections candidate lists](https://www.ncsbe.gov/results-data/candidate-lists) |
| `rcv` | Ranked-choice rounds (not candidates): Alaska and Maine races that went past round 1 | 2018–2024 | [Alaska Division of Elections](https://www.elections.alaska.gov/election-results/) RCV reports, [Maine Secretary of State](https://www.maine.gov/sos/elections-voting/election-results-data) RCV summaries |
| `va` | Virginia: federal, General Assembly specials, city and town councils, mayors, school boards, boards of supervisors, constitutional officers | 2026–present | [Virginia Department of Elections candidate lists](https://www.elections.virginia.gov/casting-a-ballot/candidate-list/) |

There is no free national list of *upcoming* state and local candidates. `medsl` gives every state's state and local races for past general elections. Official candidate lists for upcoming elections (`nc`, `md`, `va`) are added one state at a time, and `enr` reads the 2026 candidates off six states' election-night results files, which list every contest before any votes are counted. See [Adding a source](#adding-a-source). For the other states, `civic` looks up the ballots of sampled addresses in Google's Civic Information API.

### Caveats

- **FEC data covers registrations, not ballots.** Anyone who files a federal Statement of Candidacy appears in it, including many people who never qualify for a ballot. Use `candidate_status` (`statutory_candidate` means they crossed the FEC's $5,000 threshold) to filter. A cycle file such as `fec/2026.csv` holds elections in 2025–2026; odd years are special elections.
- **MD files list each primary and general election's candidates.** Maryland renames its files every cycle, so each new election needs an entry in `ELECTIONS` in `sources/md.py`. Lists from before 2022 have no header row and aren't supported. City races are mostly absent, because cities run their own elections.
- **NC files are per calendar year** and include every primary, general and municipal election held that year.
- **MEDSL rows are general-election results reduced to candidates.** They show who appeared on the November ballot, not who filed: primary losers and withdrawn candidates are absent, and there is no 2026 data until MEDSL publishes it. Local coverage depends on what each state reports. Some state-years have no local races (for example California 2018 and New York), and a few files give only surnames (Texas 2022 and 2024). Write-ins and turnout lines are dropped. Statewide ballot measures go to the results files for the map (see [Results map](#results-map)), not the candidate lists; local measures are dropped. Candidates in Georgia's December 2022 runoffs appear a second time, with `election_type` `runoff`. Candidates on several party lines (fusion voting) get one row, with the parties joined by `/`. Local races carry the county as `jurisdiction`, or the town in New England. MEDSL now publishes 2020 only on Harvard Dataverse, whose downloads need a form with your name and email, so 2020 comes from the last copy it kept on GitHub: state and federal offices only (no local races), and no Indiana, which MEDSL added later. 2016 comes from MEDSL's older precinct files, which have no election dates and spell offices and names as each county did; a statewide candidate's spellings ("Moran, Jerry", "Jerry Moran") are merged using MEDSL's normalized surname. The daily refresh skips this source because the data is historical. Re-pull it with `fetch --all-years --source medsl`.
- **`civic` ballots are sampled, so they're incomplete.** Google's API only answers "what is on this address's ballot", so the source looks up a street address for each county: the county government's, then its cities', townships' and school districts' in proportion to population, from the Census Bureau's [2022 list of governments](https://www.census.gov/data/datasets/2022/econ/gus/public-use-files.html), up to 4,000 lookups a run. A race is found only if a sampled address votes in it, so many city, ward, school board and special-district races are missed, and about 270 of the roughly 3,000 counties and county-equivalents get no lookup because all their listed addresses are PO boxes. Google also has only what each state sends the Voting Information Project, which may be polling places without contests, and it may not answer for addresses where nobody is registered (such as offices). Races that cross county lines list the counties they were found in, except state legislative districts, which use Census lines as below. Local races carry the sampled county as `jurisdiction` (Connecticut's are its planning regions). Federal races are left to `fec` and ballot measures are skipped. North Carolina, Maryland and Virginia are skipped because `nc`, `md` and `va` cover them. Candidate status, incumbency and IDs aren't provided. Google's terms allow keeping contest data for at most 24 hours, so the daily refresh replaces `civic/<year>.csv`, and once Google stops serving an election the file is deleted. The source needs a `GOOGLE_CIVIC_API_KEY` (see [Data refresh](#data-refresh)); without one it is skipped.
- **District counties come from Census Bureau lines.** Candidate lists name a district but not the counties it spans, so FEC House races and Maryland congressional and legislative races get `counties` from the [Census Bureau's district-to-county relationship files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/time-series/geo/relationship-files.html). Each election uses the lines it was held on: the 109th Congress for 2004, the 110th for 2006–2010, the 113th for 2012–2014, the 115th for 2016, the 116th for 2018–2020, the 118th for 2022 and the 119th for 2024. A district is listed in every county it shares land with, even a sliver. These are left empty because no published lines match them: 2000 and 2002, North Carolina in 2020 (court-ordered map), and in 2026 the ten states with new maps (AL, CA, FL, LA, MO, NC, OH, TN, TX, UT). Every other state's 2026 race uses its 2024 lines. Update `REDRAWN` in `districts.py` if a state's map changes. Maryland circuit-court judges are listed by circuit but elected county by county, so they're placed in the judge's county. Maryland appellate judges stay unplaced.
- **Virginia's list is one row per candidate per locality.** ELECT's workbook repeats a race in every county and independent city it's on the ballot in, so `va` joins localities that list the same candidates for the same office and district into one race, with those localities as `counties` (a town in two counties, a sheriff shared by a city and a county). Same-titled races in different cities ("Member City Council", "District 2") stay apart because their candidates differ. When a town race names the town only in the district column ("Mayor", "Farmville"), the town is added to the office ("Mayor - Farmville"). Most local candidates are listed as `Independent` (`IND`), as on Virginia's ballot. `incumbent_status` comes from the list's Incumbent column; in races with several seats, non-incumbents are left blank because a seat may be open. The workbook's campaign emails, phones and addresses are never read. Its name changes with each revision, so the source follows the link on the election's list page; add each new election's page to `ELECTIONS` in `sources/va.py`.
- **Ranked-choice rounds are a separate file.** Alaska (since 2022) and Maine (since 2018) count some races by ranked choice. MEDSL has only first choices, so `rcv` adds each round from the states' official tabulations to `data/rounds/rcv/<year>.csv`: `state`, `election_year`, `election_date`, `election_type`, `office`, `district`, `round`, `candidate_name`, `party`, `votes`, `source`. Each round also has an `(exhausted)` line: ballots that counted for a candidate in round 1 but rank nobody still in the count. Office and district are spelled the way MEDSL spells them. `party` is usually empty, because most reports don't give it. Only races that went past round 1 have rounds. Covered: Alaska's 2022 general (federal and legislative, plus the August 2022 special US House race) and 2024 general; Maine's 2018 and 2022 2nd District general, 2018 Democratic primaries for governor and the 2nd District, and 2020 primaries. Maine's 2024 2nd District summary is a scanned image with no text, so it's missing, as are Maine primaries since 2022, which are PDFs this hasn't been taught to read. Each new election's report URLs must be added to `REPORTS` in `sources/rcv.py`; the daily refresh doesn't pull this source. Use `fetch --all-years --source rcv`.
- **US territories have only their House seat.** Puerto Rico, Guam, the US Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands appear in the state picker with their FEC candidates for the House: `US House Resident Commissioner` for Puerto Rico (a four-year term, elected in presidential years, so there is none in 2026) and `US House Delegate` for the others and DC. Territory governor, legislature and local races aren't covered, and there are no territory results: MEDSL covers only the 50 states and DC, Puerto Rico's election commission (CEE) blocks automated downloads, and the other territories publish candidate lists and results as PDFs or web pages rather than data files. On the results map the territories are a row of tiles under the country, marked "No results" (or "no such race" for president and US Senate, which territories don't vote for).
- **`enr` results are unofficial and incomplete until certified.** They are election-night counts, refreshed every 10 minutes from Nov 3 to Nov 6, 2026, then daily. They change as counting goes on, and "precincts reporting" says little in mostly-mail states such as Colorado. Only statewide races are kept, write-ins are dropped, and nothing is written for a state until it reports votes. Florida's and Minnesota's files don't say when they were last updated, so the map shows no time for them. States are listed in `FEEDS` in `sources/enr.py`, with each Clarity site's election ID; a blank ID is looked up in the site's `elections.json` by date. Once MEDSL's certified results cover a state and year, the map uses those instead.
- **`enr` candidates are whatever the state's results file carries.** That's every state, legislative and judicial race on the 2026 general ballot, but only the local races the state's own site reports: Minnesota's county offices and soil and water districts, and a few town, school and special-district races in Colorado, Florida and South Carolina (each state's counties post the rest on their own sites). Federal races are left to `fec`, and ballot questions and retention votes are skipped. A race on every county's ballot is statewide; the others list the counties the file gives, and a local race in one county names it as `jurisdiction`. Florida gives tickets by surname ("Donalds / Avila") and marks qualified write-in candidates, which get `candidate_status` `write-in` and no party. Incumbency isn't given. The daily refresh pulls these until election day, the live-results workflow (which commits only results) during the count, and the daily refresh again after it.
- **No contact details are published.** Sources include candidates' street addresses, phone numbers and emails. The pipeline deliberately drops them.

## Columns

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `state` | Two-letter code of the office's state (`US` for president) |
| `election_year` | Year of the election |
| `election_date` | ISO date, when the source provides it |
| `level` | `federal`, `state` or `local` |
| `office` | Office or contest name as the source words it (FEC offices normalized to `US House` / `US Senate` / `US President`; DC's and the territories' House members are `US House Delegate`, Puerto Rico's `US House Resident Commissioner`) |
| `district` | District or seat identifier, when there is one |
| `jurisdiction` | County (MEDSL: town in New England), when the race is confined to one. Wording varies by source, e.g. `WAKE` vs `Allegany County` |
| `counties` | Counties whose ballot has the race, `;`-separated (e.g. `DURHAM;WAKE`). `*` means every county in the state. District races are filled in from Census district lines (see Caveats). Empty means nobody says, e.g. 2026 House races in states with new maps |
| `candidate_name` | Name as the source gives it (FEC: `LAST, FIRST`; NC: as printed on the ballot) |
| `party` | Party code from the source (e.g. `DEM`, `REP`, `LIB`, `GRE`, `UNA`) |
| `election_type` | `primary`, `general`, `runoff`, or empty when the source doesn't say |
| `incumbent_status` | `incumbent`, `challenger`, `open_seat` (FEC only) |
| `candidate_status` | Source-specific status (FEC: registration status; MD: `active`, `withdrawn`, `disqualified`, …) |
| `source` | Which source the row came from |
| `source_candidate_id` | The source's ID for the candidate (FEC `CAND_ID`) |

An empty value always means "the source didn't provide it". The pipeline never guesses.

## Running it

Requires [uv](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/).

```sh
uv sync
uv run who-is-running sources                         # list sources
uv run who-is-running fetch --refresh                 # re-pull recent years for every source
uv run who-is-running fetch --years 2026 --source nc  # one source, one year
uv run who-is-running fetch --all-years               # full historical backfill
```

## Data refresh

[`.github/workflows/refresh.yml`](.github/workflows/refresh.yml) runs daily, re-pulls each source's current and previous year, and commits any changes to `main`. To backfill history, run it manually with `mode = all-years`. If one source fails, the others still commit, and the run is then marked failed.

The `civic` source needs a Google API key, stored as the repository secret `GOOGLE_CIVIC_API_KEY`: in the Google Cloud console, enable the "Google Civic Information API", create an API key restricted to it, then `gh secret set GOOGLE_CIVIC_API_KEY`. Locally, set the same environment variable. For now only manual runs (`gh workflow run refresh.yml -f source=civic`) pass the key, so the daily run skips `civic`. A run uses up to about 4,000 of the free 25,000 daily requests. Google also caps requests per minute. When it answers 429 (over quota), the source pauses for a minute and halves its rate, and after 40 minutes it stops looking up new addresses and keeps what it found, so a low per-minute quota means fewer counties sampled. The quota is listed in the Cloud console under the API's **Quotas** tab, and the source's log line reports how many lookups were skipped.

On election night, [`.github/workflows/live-results.yml`](.github/workflows/live-results.yml) re-pulls only the live `enr` results every 10 minutes, from 23:00 UTC on Nov 3 2026 until the end of Nov 6 (UTC), and redeploys the site whenever they change. It does nothing outside that window unless run by hand. The daily refresh leaves `enr` alone during that window and picks it up afterwards.

## Website

The site is static: plain HTML, CSS and JS in [`web/`](web/), with no framework or build tooling. `who-is-running build-site` copies `web/` to `_site/`. It converts each CSV to compact JSON, one file per state (`data/<source>/<year>/<state>.json`), and writes `data/manifest.json`, which lists every file and the states and years it covers, so the page downloads only the state it shows. Where sources overlap, the page by default prefers an official candidate list, then MEDSL results, then FEC registrations. [`.github/workflows/pages.yml`](.github/workflows/pages.yml) deploys to GitHub Pages on every push to `main` and after every data refresh.

### Results map

Above the candidate search, a map shows who won each state in past November general elections (2016 to 2024, from MEDSL): president, US Senate or governor, shaded by the winner's party and margin (under 5, 5–10, 10–20 and 20+ points). Pick a state, on the map or in the state picker, to see that state's counties for any of its statewide races, with each county's winner, margin and top candidates on hover or tap. A table view lists the same numbers. The five US territories sit in a row of tiles under the country map; we have no results for them yet (see Caveats).

Where a state also held a runoff or primary for a statewide race, an **Election** picker appears for that state: General, Runoff, Primary or Primary runoff. Each is its own contest, with its own totals. A primary is one party's contest, so its counties are colored by winning candidate instead of party: the three candidates with the most votes statewide get their own colors, named in the legend and tooltip, and counties won by anyone else share a gray hatch. Primary maps aren't shaded by margin; the tooltip and table give it.

- County totals are summed from MEDSL's precinct returns, using each precinct's `TOTAL` row when it has one and otherwise adding up its vote modes.
- **Runoffs:** MEDSL's November files include only one statewide runoff: Georgia's December 6, 2022 US Senate runoff. Other runoffs (Georgia 2018 Secretary of State, Mississippi 2018 Senate, Louisiana's December elections) aren't in them. A few counties' recounts (Arizona 2022 attorney general, Montana 2018, New Hampshire) are left out, so those maps show the original count.
- **Primaries:** only 2018, from MEDSL's separate [official primary returns](https://github.com/MEDSL/primary-precinct-returns), and only for the states whose statewide totals matched their certified results (`PRIMARY_STATES` in `sources/medsl.py`). MEDSL has no primary returns for 2022 or 2024. Primary votes appear on the map only; the candidate lists still show general-election ballots.
- Lines that repeat a county's precincts as one more precinct (Idaho 2022's `COUNTY TOTAL`, Michigan 2022's `TOTALS`) are skipped, so those counties aren't counted twice. Statewide court seats that MEDSL files as districts, such as North Carolina's 2018 Supreme Court race (`001`), are mapped as "SEAT 1" and so on.
- A race MEDSL's file misses counties for is left off the map rather than drawn wrong, except where `sources/medsl_fill.py` replaces it with the state's certified county results: California 2022 US Senate, regular and special (Secretary of State's Statement of Vote), Tennessee 2022 governor (Secretary of State), Idaho 2022 US Senate and every statewide office (Secretary of State canvass; MEDSL has no votes for Power County in most of them), Indiana 2022 US Senate, secretary of state, auditor and treasurer (Election Division), and North Dakota 2024 governor ([OpenElections](https://github.com/openelections/openelections-data-nd)' copy of the Secretary of State's results, since the state's site keeps only its latest election). The candidate list still comes from MEDSL, so California's 2022 Senate candidates and Tennessee's 2022 governor candidates appear on the map but not in the list.
- Still missing: New York 2018 governor, US Senate, attorney general and comptroller, and New York 2022 US Senate and comptroller. MEDSL's files miss counties, and the State Board of Elections' site answers scripts with a browser challenge, so there's no fill yet.
- Some votes aren't reported by county (most of Alaska's early and absentee ballots, for example). They count toward the statewide result, and the map's note says what share the county shading leaves out. Kansas City, Missouri reports apart from its county, but only for the part of the city in Jackson County, so it's drawn as part of Jackson County. Kalawao County, Hawaii has no polling place; its votes are counted with Maui County, and the map says so.
- **Ranked-choice races** (Alaska, Maine) show who won after all the rounds. A state decided by ranked choice is shaded by its final-round winner and margin, and its tooltip says so. Its counties keep their first choices, because the later rounds are only counted statewide; the note under the map says this. A table of each round sits under the map, along with the state's other ranked-choice counts that year (district races and primaries), and the same table is in each matching race in the candidate list.
- **Ballot measures.** A state's statewide ballot measures are listed under "Ballot measures" in its race picker. Counties are shaded by whether Yes or No leads, and by how far, using the same margin steps in green (Yes) and violet (No), so a measure never looks like a party. In the results CSV a measure is a race whose candidates are `Yes` and `No`, with no party; MEDSL's many wordings (`FOR`/`AGAINST`, `APPROVE`/`REJECT`, `YES FOR APPROVAL`, …) are read as Yes or No. The title is MEDSL's `office` text, which for some states is just a number (California's `PROPOSITION 1`).
- A measure is marked passed or failed only where one rule covers every statewide measure that year: a simple majority of Yes and No votes in most listed states, and 60% in Florida. `PASS_RULES` in `measures.py` lists them. Elsewhere only the split is shown: Wyoming, Minnesota, Hawaii and Tennessee count against all ballots cast in the election (Wyoming's 2022 Amendment A drew more Yes than No votes and still failed), and Colorado, Illinois and New Hampshire need supermajorities for some measures. Passed and failed describe the vote; courts have later voided measures that passed, such as Kentucky's 2018 victims' rights amendment.
- A measure is kept only when MEDSL has it in every county. That leaves out a few measures where a file misses a county or spells it differently (New York's 2024 Proposal 1 lacks Herkimer; Virginia's 2018 amendments call King and Queen County `KING & QUEEN`), Georgia 2022 (it spells each measure two ways in different counties), and measures MEDSL didn't include at all (Michigan 2022 and 2024, Massachusetts, Nebraska 2024, and four of California's seven 2022 propositions, for example). Judicial retention votes and Washington's advisory votes (Repealed/Maintained) aren't measures here.
- MEDSL's precinct files can differ slightly from certified totals (2020 president: within 0.01% nationally). 2020 has no Indiana (see the MEDSL caveat above), so the map hatches it as having no results. In 2016, New Hampshire's city wards have no county in MEDSL's file, so each city's wards are filed under the county the city lies in, and Vermont's presidential lines have no party, so Clinton and Trump are given theirs. A race whose file gives no district but where no candidate got votes in at least 90% of the counties it was on the ballot in (2016's State House in New Hampshire and Vermont, Maryland 2018's circuit court clerks) is really a set of district races, so it isn't mapped. There are no results before 2016. For 2026, election-night results from the `enr` source show for the states it covers, labelled unofficial, with the share of precincts reporting and when the state last updated them; each county's tooltip gives its precincts reporting. When MEDSL publishes certified 2026 results, they replace the live ones state by state.
- Shapes come from [us-atlas](https://github.com/topojson/us-atlas) (Census Bureau 1:10M boundaries, ISC license). `who-is-running build-geo` regenerates `web/geo/` from it.

## Adding a source

1. Create `src/who_is_running/sources/<state>.py` with a `Source` subclass. It implements `available_years`, `refresh_years` and `candidacies(year)`, which returns `Candidacy` records. Accept the injected `fetch` callable instead of calling the network directly.
2. Register it in `src/who_is_running/sources/__init__.py`, and add a short label in `SOURCE_LABELS` in `web/app.js` and a link in the footer of `web/index.html`.
3. Add a small fixture made from real rows to `tests/fixtures/`, plus tests that use `FakeWeb`.

Good candidates are states whose election office publishes a statewide, machine-readable candidate list that includes local races at a stable URL.
